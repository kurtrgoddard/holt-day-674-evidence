I wanted to know whether Susan Holt’s current polling lead puts her government on a clear re-election path, or whether it resembles the mid-mandate advantages New Brunswick governments have lost before. I ended up building the evidence trail rather than guessing. As of August 26, 2026, mandate day 674: • A recency-weighted benchmark from three public polls put the Liberals at 43.2%, the PCs at 33.0% and the Greens at 13.4%. • A transparent uniform proportional-swing exercise produced 26 to 32 Liberal seats across the three polls. Each scenario cleared the 25-seat majority line, but this is a deterministic stress test, not a probability forecast. • Shawn Graham, David Alward and Brian Gallant each led at approximately this mandate age and later lost government. Facebook post

I have been trying to answer a simple New Brunswick political question: does Susan Holt’s current lead mean her government is safe? The evidence supports a narrower answer. As of August 26, 2026, the recency-weighted Liberal benchmark was 43.2%, compared with 33.0% for the PCs and 13.4% for the Greens. All three current deterministic seat scenarios produced Liberal majorities, ranging from 26 to 32 seats. That is a stress test of an election held then, not a probability forecast. History supplies a warning, not a verdict. Graham, Alward and Gallant each led at approximately this mandate age and later lost government. Higgs was re-elected, but his comparison occurred only 47 days before a pandemic-era snap election. The policy picture is also mixed. Implementation is documented in areas such as collaborative-care clinics, housing starts and school breakfast. Evidence of resident-level outcomes and public recognition remains more limited. The most useful next question is what residents know, experience and attribute to the government, measured in the same survey. Five short responses to predictable pushback

“You’re carrying water for the Liberals.” / “This is a PC hit job.”
Same yardstick for all five premiers, all sources public, every claim numbered. If a specific figure is wrong, point at it and I’ll correct it in the log.

“So you’re saying Holt loses.”
No. Three premiers lost from this position and one didn’t. The record is a warning about lead durability, not a verdict, and the analysis refuses to assign a 2028 probability in either direction.

“Polls are junk.”
These three are opt-in panels with real limitations, which is why the lead is shown as a range (+5 to +15), the bases are disclosed, and the seat numbers are labelled scenarios rather than forecasts.

“Every serious model publishes a probability. Why won’t you?”
A calibrated probability needs enough comparable historical cases to test against. New Brunswick offers three usable ones. Publishing a percentage on that base would be precision theatre, so the gate stays closed.

“The government has delivered plenty — this is unfair.” / “They’ve delivered nothing.”
The audit found both partisan versions wrong: implementation is real (15 clinics, 7,587 housing starts, universal breakfast) and outcome evidence is thin (primary-care attachment fell; completions and participation data don’t exist yet). Both halves are in the same graphic on purpose.
